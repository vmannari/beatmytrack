Legal review — Beat My Track Terms & Conditions
Not yet publication-ready
Strong legal foundation

My assessment: the draft is substantially better than typical indie-game terms. It protects the publisher's legitimate interests without relying heavily on exclusions that would probably be unenforceable under EU consumer law.

However, I would not approve this version for publication yet. There are several unresolved commercial terms and some important gaming-platform obligations that need attention.

I'm assessing this from the perspective of a Finnish game publisher distributing internationally, initially through Google Play, with user-created tracks, online competition, advertising, and in-app purchases. This is a specialist-style legal risk review, not a formal opinion from a retained Finnish lawyer.
1. Critical issues to resolve before launch
Priority	Section	Assessment

Critical	§8 — Purchases	Publishing slots and ad-free entitlements are not sufficiently defined to form reliable purchase agreements.
Critical	§9 — Refunds	The legal framework is correct, but the withdrawal procedure and treatment of individual products are unfinished.

A. Age and minors — §3
Your intended audience includes teenagers, making this a real legal issue rather than a formality.

There are three different legal questions:
- Access to the game: What minimum age will you allow?
- Personal-data processing: In Finland, the age for a child to consent independently to processing based on GDPR Article 8 is 13. Other countries have different thresholds. This is not a general minimum age for using the game. 
  
  
  tietosuoja.fi
- Purchases: Finnish minors generally cannot make purchases beyond ordinary, minor transactions without the necessary authorisation. The total value of repeated in-game purchases matters.

I recommend deciding the intended minimum age before finalising the terms, privacy policy or Google Play audience settings.

If you allow 15–17-year-olds, the purchasing and advertising arrangements must account for them.
B. In-app purchases — §8
This is the biggest commercial drafting weakness.

The document lists five product types but leaves essential properties unresolved.

For publishing slots, the contract must explain:
- Whether a slot is permanently consumed by publishing or can be reused.
- Whether removing a track restores the slot.
- What happens when a published track is moderated.
- What happens to purchased slots after account deletion or service shutdown.
- How purchased slots interact with the monthly free slot, which does not accumulate.
For ad-free purchases, the player must know exactly which advertisements disappear.

Particular concern: If rewarded advertisements remain necessary to obtain extra resumes, describing the purchase simply as "ad-free" may mislead players.

A safer product name could be Remove automatic ads, with an explanation that optional rewarded advertisements remain available. Alternatively, you can design the benefit to remove all advertising requirements.

Google Play also requires accurate disclosure of digital purchase features and pricing.

C. Withdrawal and refunds — §9
The section correctly preserves statutory consumer rights. Nevertheless, more precise treatment is needed for:
- Immediately delivered digital content.
- Continuing digital services.
- One-month ad-free benefits.
- Purchases that have already been partly used.
These categories can have different withdrawal consequences.

An important development is already in force: since 19 June 2026, qualifying online contracts must support an accessible electronic withdrawal function where the consumer has a withdrawal right. The function must be available through the interface used to conclude the contract, subject to the applicable requirements. 


KKV



For Google Play purchases, you need to verify the responsibility and available functionality of the actual checkout provider. For any direct checkout you operate, the process is your responsibility.

Do not activate sales until the product-specific withdrawal classification, checkout disclosures, confirmations and refund handling are established.
D. User-generated content and moderation — §7 and §11
Beat My Track allows players to publish tracks, choose names, share challenges and participate in online competitions.

That means the game may qualify as a hosting service under the EU Digital Services Act (DSA). Because public track discovery and sharing are central features, it may also qualify as an online platform. The exact classification needs a technical and legal assessment. 


Traficom



Even small hosting providers have obligations concerning illegal-content reporting, moderation decisions and contact channels. Micro and small enterprises receive exemptions from certain additional platform obligations, but not all hosting obligations. 


Traficom



The draft covers much of this conceptually. What remains important is the actual implementation:
- Reporting and processing illegal-content notices.
- Giving legally required reasons for removals and restrictions.
- Offering the applicable review or appeal procedures.
- Recording moderation decisions and evidence.
- Providing in-game reporting and blocking functionality appropriate to the game's social features.
Google Play independently requires effective moderation and accessible reporting/blocking mechanisms for user-generated content.

E. Permanent purchases and shutdown rights — §14
This is the provision I would scrutinise most carefully for fairness.

The draft gives the publisher broad discretion to shut down the service, including because of personal circumstances or a decision to pursue other activities.

It correctly preserves statutory remedies, but that alone may not make the contractual arrangement fair.

For example, a player buying permanent ad removal could reasonably expect more than a benefit that disappears shortly after purchase at the publisher's discretion.

Finnish consumer guidance specifically warns about unilateral changes without proper grounds and termination of ongoing contracts without sufficient notice. 


KKV



My recommendation:
1. Retain the right to discontinue commercially unsustainable services.
2. Define a reasonable notice policy for planned shutdowns.
3. Explain what happens to purchased entitlements and user-created tracks.
4. Apply statutory refunds or other remedies where required.
5. Avoid implying that purchasing a permanent benefit provides lifetime server hosting.
I would also remove the explicit reference to the publisher's personal circumstances from the consumer-facing terms. It adds little useful protection and makes the provision appear unnecessarily discretionary.

A particular recommendation about player-created tracks
I would separate the intellectual-property rights into three categories:
1. Your assets: Road segments, vehicles, scenery, textures, sounds and game software remain yours.
2. Player contributions: Original track layouts, original names and other creative contributions remain the player's, to the extent they attract legal protection.
3. Usage permissions: Players grant the limited permissions necessary to operate the game, distribute shared tracks, show leaderboard content and allow other players to race those tracks.
This distinction is particularly important if you eventually sell the game business.

I would also decide whether you're permitted to use exceptional player-created tracks in promotional material. The current licence may not adequately authorise marketing use outside operating the game.
3. Wording I would specifically change
§14 — Discontinuation
Replace or narrow
The current broad wording about stopping maintenance for personal reasons makes it harder to defend the fairness of permanent purchases.

Suggested approach:
We may discontinue online services for legitimate operational, technical or commercial reasons, subject to applicable contractual obligations and consumer rights. For planned discontinuation, we will normally provide at least [60 days] advance notice, except where an urgent legal, security or comparable reason requires earlier action. We will explain the consequences for purchased benefits and player-created content and provide any remedies required by law.

60 days is a proposed commercial commitment, not a universal statutory minimum. Choose a period you can reliably honour.

§15 — Bugs
Simplify
I would delete "as is" and "as available". These expressions add little practical protection under Finnish consumer law.

Suggested approach:
We aim to provide a reliable and secure gaming experience, but cannot guarantee uninterrupted availability or that the game will be entirely free of defects. This does not affect our responsibility to deliver the content and services agreed upon or your statutory rights concerning defects and remedies.



§8 — Purchased publishing slots
Complete before sales
The final language depends on whether publishing slots are reusable capacity or single-use allocations.

The contract should explicitly state:
Each purchased publishing slot allows [DEFINED PUBLICATION RIGHT]. A slot [CAN/CANNOT] be reused after a published track is removed. Purchased slots [DO/DO NOT] expire while the relevant service remains available. If a track is removed for violating the community rules, the affected slot will [DEFINED CONSEQUENCE], subject to applicable consumer rights.



4. Clauses I would largely keep
Several sections are already well judged.
§2 — Mandatory consumer rights

Correctly prevents the terms from overriding statutory protections.


§6 — Competition integrity

Provides reasonable authority to investigate cheating without requiring evidence to be conclusive before temporary action.


§11 — Account bans

The proportionality, evidence, appeals and paid-benefit safeguards are thoughtfully drafted. I would preserve this approach.


§13 — Game updates

Allows substantial changes to gameplay, physics, rankings and progression while recognising legal limits on changes to paid services.


§16–17 — Liability and disputes

Sensible allocation of responsibility and a Finnish governing-law clause that preserves mandatory foreign consumer protections.


§19 — Sale or transfer of the business

Particularly valuable for an independent developer. It anticipates future business transfers without claiming ownership of player-created material.



5. The terms are only one part of compliance
Some important protections must be implemented in the game or backend.
Requirement	Implementation needed
Acceptance	Record terms version and acceptance before relevant online interactions.
Purchases	Verify orders, preserve entitlement records, and reconcile refunds and reversals.
Withdrawals	Implement applicable checkout disclosures, confirmations and withdrawal handling.
Moderation	Retain decisions, supporting evidence and reasons, with an appropriate appeal process.
Privacy	Implement actual consent management and age-appropriate advertising controls.
Account deletion	Warn about consequences, process deletion and handle legally required data retention.
Shutdown	Stop sales when benefits can no longer be supplied and provide appropriate notice and data retrieval.

For the Google Cloud backend, I would treat these as acceptance criteria for the account, commerce and moderation services.
6. Final recommendation
My recommended order is:
1. Finalise commercial decisions: Exact publishing-slot behaviour, ad-free scope, purchase recovery and shutdown commitments.
2. Finalise eligibility: Minimum age, parental requirements and intended countries.
3. Complete compliance processes: Checkout, refunds, account deletion and moderation.
4. Update the terms: Incorporate the agreed behaviour and remove unnecessary legalistic repetition.
5. Obtain final Finnish legal sign-off: Including the privacy policy, community rules and actual purchase screens.
One final observation: the draft is quite long and defensive in places. I would retain its legal protections while making it easier for a player to understand. A game targeting teenagers and young adults benefits from plain-language terms with clear commercial descriptions.

Bottom line: I would keep the overall legal structure. The most important work is resolving the meaning of paid entitlements, tightening shutdown rights, establishing the age policy, and ensuring the legal promises match the implemented systems. The underlying consumer-rights, moderation and business-transfer clauses are already on a sound track.
